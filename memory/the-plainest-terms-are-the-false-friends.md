**The official glossary does not assume the ordinary dictionary sense of its headwords. It
silently replaces it, and never once warns that it has.** That is the single most useful
thing to know about reading it.

It is not a dictionary and not a language built from scratch. It is a **precising**
lexicon: it takes an existing English word and fixes a technical value for it, within a
system. The entries are genus-plus-differentia — *"A mental factor that…"*, *"The cognizer
that principally apprehends…"* — which is the English surface of the Tibetan scholastic
definitional form, where a definition supplies the criterion for applying a name to
instances. An ordinary dictionary records a family of uses; these entries answer *what
class contains this, what object does it take, what does it cause, what contrasts with
it*. A dictionary cannot tell a reader that feeling accompanies **every** primary mind.
Its position in the system does.

**The definitions are recursive, not self-contained.** They assume ordinary English for
their connective tissue — *makes*, *wishes*, *located*, *undesirable* — while the nouns
inside them are themselves technical nodes: to read *anger* you need *deluded mental
factor* and *contaminated object*; to read *effort* you need the technical sense of
*virtue*. It is an ontology of linked nodes. The glossary is an index to a corpus, not a
replacement for one.

## The rule this yields

1. A displayed headword always carries the technical sense.
2. A term used classificatorily **inside** another definition also carries it.
3. Ordinary connective English stays ordinary — do not apply a headword's specialist sense
   to every occurrence of the same string.
4. The border is a continuum, not a line. Specialized value is activated by context.

## The counterintuitive part, which is the whole point

**The dangerous terms are the plainest ones.** An odd phrase — *uncommon dominant
condition*, *non-ascertaining perceiver* — causes puzzlement, and puzzlement makes a
reader seek help. A familiar word produces *confident error* and suppresses the lookup
entirely. So the rule is not "warn on every technical term" but **warn where ordinary
competence produces confident error.**

This cuts against this collection's own instinct. [[write-plainly-for-the-reader]] says cut
your jargon and keep the tradition's exact — still right. But where the tradition's exact
word is *also* an everyday word, keeping it exact is not enough; the map has to break the
false equivalence, or the reader builds a different map from the one intended.

## The ranked list

Two rankings, because *most divergent from a dictionary* and *most destructive on a map of
the mind* are not the same question. **Emptiness** is the gravest lexical trap in the
glossary as a whole but is peripheral to the fifty-one factors; the
**awareness / consciousness** cluster is the least clean contradiction but the most
load-bearing structurally.

**Critical — warn outright:**

| Term | Ordinary sense | Technical sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Emptiness** | nothingness, void | lack of *inherent* existence — not non-existence |
| **Delusion** | a false belief, with a psychiatric shadow | the class of factors that disturb the mind and rob it of control; includes attachment and anger, which are not beliefs at all |
| **Feeling** | emotion, mood | *only* the pleasant / unpleasant / neutral tone |
| **Discrimination** | prejudicial treatment — now the dominant reflex | distinguishing an object by its characteristic sign; present in every mind |
| **Contact** | touch | perceives the object as pleasant/unpleasant/neutral, and so is the basis of feeling |
| **Attachment** | affection, healthy bonding | always a delusion here |
| **Mindfulness** | present-centred non-judgmental awareness | not forgetting the object — nearer recollection |
| **non-** prefix trio | mere absence, indifference | *positive* virtuous opponents |
| **Mentality** | outlook, mindset | a synonym for primary mind |
| **awareness / consciousness** | no stable English rule, but many readers assume consciousness is the container | **inverted**: awareness is the wider class; consciousness means only primary minds |

**Major:** intention (broader than a conscious plan), effort (delights in virtue, not
strain), contaminated (gives rise to delusions, not polluted), sense/mental power (a
dominant condition, not an ability or an organ). **Significant:** faith (three defined
forms, not credulity), doubt (only *deluded* doubt is the root delusion — the qualifier is
load-bearing).

**Two judgements worth recording**, because both were checked rather than assumed:

- *Discrimination* is **not** an overreaction. The source tradition does not share modern
  English's social-prejudice reflex, but the map's readers do, and that is precisely what
  makes it a translation-interface problem. An older neutral sense of "distinguishing"
  explains why the word was available to translators; it does not make an unglossed modern
  display safe.
- On *awareness/consciousness*, the reader hazard is real and belongs first — but do **not**
  claim English dictionaries encode the reverse hierarchy. They encode no dependable
  hierarchy at all, often defining consciousness *in terms of* awareness. The danger is
  that readers supply their own intuition, frequently reversed. Overstating this is easy
  and was caught.

## What may be left alone

Do not add an alarm merely because a technical definition is narrower. Aspiration,
investigation and analysis, firm apprehension, contentment, non-harmfulness, and regret
and sleep individually are clear enough once their function is visible.

## The literature, verified and citable

There is **no published translator's manual or bilingual concordance** for this English.
The terminology is demonstrably controlled, collaborative and revised, but the *rationale*
is nowhere public — do not promote inferred practice into stated policy.

- Paul J. Griffiths, "Buddhist Hybrid English: Some Notes on Philology and Hermeneutics for
  Buddhologists", *JIABS* 4.2 (1981): 17–32. Coined the term for translationese
  "comprehensible only to the initiate". His remedy is to separate philology from
  hermeneutics — **displaying a house term does not communicate the concept**, which is the
  argument for a reader's aid rather than against plain English.
- Ligeia Lugli, "Words or Terms? Models of Terminology and the Translation of Buddhist
  Sanskrit Vocabulary", in Alice Collett (ed.), *Translating Buddhism*, SUNY 2021, 149–172.
  Case study is *saṃjñā*, the term behind **discrimination**.
- Pascale Hugon, *JIABS* 32.1–2 (2009/2010): 319–368, on the Tibetan three-part theory of
  definition — why "a cognizer that…" is an inherited technology, not clumsy lexicography.
- Doboom Tulku (ed.), *Buddhist Translations: Problems and Perspectives*, Manohar 1995 —
  Napper, "Styles and Principles of Translation", 35–42; also Ruegg, Wilson, Dreyfus,
  Sharpa Tulku. Assembled **because** varied styles and unstandardised terminology were
  confusing readers.
- Bhikkhu Bodhi and Rupert Gethin, both in *Contemporary Buddhism* 12.1 (2011), on
  *mindfulness* drift; Janet Gyatso (ed.), *In the Mirror of Memory*, SUNY 1992, on *smṛti*
  as recollection.
- Lati Rinbochay & Elizabeth Napper, *Mind in Tibetan Buddhism* (1981) — an independent
  translation of **this same genre**. Useful to show the renderings are *choices*; **not**
  usable to decide a term here, per [[terms-follow-the-official-glossary]].

On *kleśa*: rendered variously *affliction*, *mental affliction*, *afflictive* or
*disturbing emotion*, *defilement*, *negative emotion* — and *delusion*. The *emotion*
family fails because ignorance, doubt and views are not emotions. The category is unified
**functionally**, by arising from inappropriate attention and disturbing the mind, not by
anything the English word means. **Fidelity does not require changing the word — it
requires glossing the collision.**

**Why:** a summary that keeps the tradition's exact vocabulary can still mislead
completely, because the reader supplies a meaning the glossary never gave. That failure is
invisible to a terminology check, which only asks whether the word matches.

**How to apply:** when a term is both technically exact and an everyday word, add the
negative clause — say what it is *not* — rather than trusting the positive definition to
displace the reader's existing sense. Settled with [[consulting-dharma-codex]] on
2026-09-10; see [[anger-and-hatred-are-both-official]] for the related case where the
tradition's own two words must both be kept.
