**The root delusion is `Anger`. Its opponent is `Non-hatred`. Both are correct, and the
mismatch is not ours to fix.**

The official glossary, in adjacent entries, uses both words:

- **Anger** is the headword for the root delusion in the formal list of mental factors —
  *"A deluded mental factor that observes its contaminated object, exaggerates its bad
  qualities, considers it to be undesirable, and wishes to harm it."* The glossary's
  **Aggression** and **Resentment** entries both derive from *"the root delusion anger"*,
  and its **Delusion** entry names the three main delusions as *ignorance, desirous
  attachment, and anger*.
- **Non-hatred** is the headword for the virtuous factor — *"the direct opponent of
  hatred."* There is **no** `Non-anger` entry, and inventing one would be a real
  terminological error, not a tidy-up. It would also break the established triad of
  virtuous roots: *non-attachment, non-hatred, non-ignorance*.
- **hatred** is also the fixed member of the **three poisons**: *attachment, hatred and
  ignorance*. Official prose moves between the two words freely and deliberately enough
  to do so inside a single explanation.

## The rule that follows

Judge each occurrence by the job it is doing, never by search-and-replace:

| Context | Word |
|---|---|
| The formal list of factors; any definition whose dependency is on the root delusion | **Anger** |
| The explicit three-poisons formula, or the three-virtuous-roots contrast | **hatred** |
| The `Non-hatred` headword and its definition | **hatred**, kept verbatim |
| Ordinary explanatory prose — *"killing a mosquito out of hatred"*, *"an object of hatred"* | leave whichever the source used |
| Any `data-term` cross-reference | target **Anger**, whatever the visible word says |

That last row is the load-bearing one. **Link text and link destination do not have to
match.** Inside the `Non-hatred` note the visible word stays *hatred* — that is the
glossary's own wording — while the link points at the `Anger` note, because a link's job
is to tell the reader which catalogued factor the prose is referring them to. Renaming the
visible word to keep it symmetrical with its destination would corrupt a quoted definition
to satisfy a mechanical instinct.

**Do not create a second `Hatred` factor.** There is exactly one enumerated afflictive
factor here and its current label is *Anger*. Equally, **do not write that anger and
hatred are exact synonyms** — the sources coordinate the words (*"the faults of anger and
hatred"*), which leaves room for ordinary distinctions of degree, and the map should not
canonise a distinction the source does not make either.

One consequence worth expecting: the crosswalk panel reads **`Non-hatred → Anger`**, which
looks wrong at a glance. It is right. The panel's other rows all name factors a reader can
find on the page, and once the card is `Anger` there is no `Hatred` note for that row to
reach — so leaving *Hatred* there invents a missing factor and breaks navigation. Prefer
the visible asymmetry to a false structural symmetry.

**Why:** this is the shape of failure this collection cannot catch by itself — a tidy
hierarchy the source does not actually make, confident and plausible and wrong. The
asymmetry records a real feature of current Kadampa English. A map should make that
feature legible through context and linking, not erase it by imposing an English symmetry
the glossary itself declines to use. It is also common in translated technical
vocabularies: one member of a traditional pair fixes under an English rendering while its
opposite is catalogued under another, and English morphology then tempts an editor to
demand *anger/non-anger*. A technical name is a label in a received lexicon, not the
result of applying a prefix.

**How to apply:** before renaming any term across a page, ask what job each occurrence is
doing, and check whether the opposite member of the pair has its own separate headword.
